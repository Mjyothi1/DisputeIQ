## 1. Speaking script (about 10 minutes, about 1,200 words)

**Slide 1: Title (30 s)**
Good morning. I'm presenting DisputeIQ, an AI-powered dispute resolution platform for card issuers. The idea fits in one line: the AI understands and drafts, deterministic code decides, and a human approves. This is a working product, and I'll show it live in a moment.

**Slide 2: Problem (50 s)**
When a customer says "I was charged twice", the bank has real work to do. Someone looks up the transaction, runs checks, picks a reason code, checks the deadline, decides, and writes two documents. Most of that effort goes to routine cases, and a missed deadline can sink a valid claim. On top of that, customers often submit incomplete complaints, so analysts spend time chasing missing details. Our taxonomy and windows are illustrative, not a card-network rulebook, and all data is synthetic.

**Slide 3: Solution (50 s)**
DisputeIQ has three parts: a customer portal, an AI agent, and a reviewer desk. Customers report problems on their own transactions and track progress. The agent investigates. Reviewers get the evidence already assembled. The key design rule is who decides what: the language model reads and writes; Python decides deadlines, eligibility, amounts, duplicates and routing; and a human approves anything that is not clear-cut.

**Slide 4: Customer portal (60 s)**
Customers sign in and see only their own cards and transactions. They pick the transaction that's wrong, or enter details manually if they can't find it. The latest change is here: for manual entry the card's last four digits are now mandatory. The Submit button stays disabled until four digits are typed, and the server also rejects a missing value with a 422 and enforces exactly four digits. Manually entered details are marked "not bank-verified" so the reviewer knows to check them.

**Slide 5: AI pipeline (70 s)**
Behind that form is a nine-step LangGraph pipeline. First we mask personal data. Then the model extracts claim facts into a validated schema. We match the complaint to a transaction, and run evidence checks: duplicate charge, strong authentication, refund issued, subscription cancelled, ATM failure, amount mismatch. The model classifies the reason code; Python checks eligibility and the deadline; policy code picks the recommendation; the model drafts the letter; and a router decides auto-resolve or human review. Every step writes an audit event. The model can only lower confidence. It can never change the action.

**Slide 6: Reviewer desk (70 s)**
The reviewer sees a queue sorted by deadline. Opening a case shows the masked complaint, the matched transaction, the evidence checks, the recommendation and the reasons it needs a person. Here's a good example: the customer says "I didn't make this payment", but the transaction was OTP-authenticated. The agent flags possible friendly fraud and recommends reject, and the reviewer decides. The panel says it plainly: this is a recommendation, nothing is sent until you decide.

**Slide 7: Need more information (80 s)**
This is the second latest change. Previously this letter always asked for merchant, amount and date. Now it asks for exactly what's missing. If we couldn't identify the transaction, it asks only for the details the customer didn't already give, plus a statement line or screenshot. If we found the transaction but the claim is thin, it asks for evidence for that dispute type. For goods not received: order number, promised delivery date, and messages with the merchant. Every letter ends with a warning: never send your full card number, CVV, PIN or OTP. That list is chosen by code, not generated by the model, so it's predictable. And it's a draft: the customer sees it in their portal once a reviewer approves.

**Slide 8: Architecture (60 s)**
The frontend is React and TypeScript on Vercel. The API is FastAPI on Render with SQLAlchemy and Alembic, on PostgreSQL with pgvector, or SQLite locally. The agent is a LangGraph graph. The model provider is pluggable: Gemini by default, with Anthropic and OpenAI as alternates, and an offline rule engine for tests. Thresholds live in one YAML file.

**Slide 9: Safety (70 s)**
A case auto-resolves only if everything holds: confidence at least 0.85, amount at most ten thousand rupees, a duplicate or ATM-cash reason code, a clean refund recommendation, no injection or friendly-fraud flag, and no other dispute on that transaction. Everything else, and every rejection, goes to a person. We mask personal data before the model, treat complaint text as untrusted, and fill amounts and dates in letters from the database. No payment is executed anywhere. DisputeIQ recommends.

**Slide 10: Verified and honest (60 s)**
We ran 413 backend and 67 frontend tests. Our offline evaluations show zero unsafe auto-resolutions across 133 cases, and all adversarial complaints were flagged. I want to be clear about limits: those accuracy numbers come from the offline rule engine, the synthetic set is circular, and we have not measured live-Gemini accuracy. We'd rather say that than overclaim.

**Slide 11: Advantages and future (40 s)**
What stands out is safety by construction, a full audit trail, two working portals on one pipeline, and evidence-specific requests that cut back-and-forth. It applies to issuers, payment apps and fintech support teams. Next steps: SSO and MFA, live-model evaluation, network rulebooks, and real email and payment integrations.

**Slide 12: Conclusion (20 s)**
A working product, engineered for trust, with two fresh improvements. Thank you. Happy to take questions.

## 3. Likely judge questions (30) with accurate answers

**Product and approach**

1. **What problem do you solve?** Card dispute handling is manual and deadline-sensitive. DisputeIQ does intake, investigation, classification, eligibility, recommendation and drafting, and puts the evidence in front of a human reviewer.
2. **Does the AI make the final decision?** No. A reviewer approves everything except clear-cut, low-value duplicate/ATM refunds. Even those are bounded by code.
3. **What is auto-resolved?** Only when confidence >= 0.85, amount <= INR 10,000, reason code is DUPLICATE_CHARGE or ATM_CASH_NOT_DISPENSED, the recommendation is REFUND with checks clear, and no injection, friendly-fraud or other-dispute flag. Thresholds are in `backend/app/rules/reason_codes.yaml`.
4. **Does it actually refund money?** No. No payment or refund integration exists. Letters say a refund is "authorised", with the bank paying separately, and never that money moved.
5. **What changed most recently?** Card last 4 became mandatory for manual entry (frontend and backend), and the need-more-info letter became dynamic with a security warning.

**The latest changes**

6. **Where is last 4 enforced?** Frontend: `ReportProblemPage.tsx` keeps Submit disabled until the field has 4 characters and sends `card_last4`. Backend: `portal.py` returns HTTP 422 for manual entry without it, and the schema requires `^\d{4}$`.
7. **Is it required for listed transactions?** No. When the customer picks one of their own transactions, the card comes from the database, so it isn't asked for.
8. **Any gap in that validation?** Yes, one we know of: the frontend digit filter has an escaped-regex typo (`/\\D/g`), so letters aren't stripped as typed. The length check still applies, and the server rejects non-digits with a 422. It is a small fix.
9. **How does the need-more-info email decide what to ask?** `info_needed_text()` in `documents.py`. No transaction found: it asks for whichever of merchant, amount, date and card the customer didn't provide, plus a statement line/screenshot and why the charge is wrong. Transaction found: it uses a per-reason-code list (goods not received, not as described, cancelled recurring, incorrect amount, refund not processed, duplicate, ATM, fraud).
10. **Is that list written by the LLM?** No. It is deterministic. The model drafts prose with placeholders, and the list fills a placeholder, so the requests are predictable. Model text that contains literal amounts or dates is rejected.
11. **Is the email actually sent?** No. It is a letter draft shown to the reviewer; after approval the customer reads it in the portal. SMTP code in the repo is only for the earlier verification-code feature.
12. **Weakness of that letter?** When the customer already described the problem, "why you believe the charge is wrong" can be redundant. A smarter version could check the description first.

**AI and pipeline**

13. **Which model do you use?** Gemini Flash via `google-genai` is the configured default; Anthropic, OpenAI and an offline rule engine are alternates. Check the case page ("Analysis model") for what the deployed instance used.
14. **Why LangGraph?** Explicit nodes and state make the pipeline auditable and testable; each node writes an audit event.
15. **How do you stop hallucinated amounts in letters?** The model uses placeholders such as `{amount}`; code fills them from the database; text with literal money, dates or long numbers is rejected and a template is used.
16. **How do you handle prompt injection?** Complaint text is delimited as data, scanned by a regex detector (a hit forces human review), and LLM output is schema-validated. Policy, amounts, deadlines and routing come from code, so a hijacked model can't create a refund the evidence doesn't support. Detection is heuristic, not a guarantee.
17. **What if the LLM fails?** Errors are logged and the case goes to a human; it never silently falls back to mock.
18. **What is the model allowed to influence?** Extraction, reason-code classification, the rationale and the letter prose. Final confidence is the minimum of policy cap, classifier confidence, rationale confidence and match cap, so it can lower confidence but not raise it.
19. **How does it match a complaint to a transaction?** By amount (5% tolerance), merchant and date within a 14-day window, with a minimum match score of 0.5. A weak or ambiguous match caps confidence and forces human review.
20. **What are the reason codes?** Eight: fraud not authorised, duplicate charge, goods not received, not as described, cancelled recurring, incorrect amount, refund not processed, ATM cash not dispensed. Illustrative, not a network rulebook.
21. **What is "friendly fraud" handling?** If a customer claims fraud but the transaction was strongly authenticated (OTP/PIN), the evidence check fails the claim, the agent flags it and recommends reject for human review.

**Accuracy and evidence**

22. **What is your accuracy?** We don't claim a live-model accuracy. Offline rule engine: 99% on 100 synthetic cases (circular, because the classifier shares keywords with the templates), and 83.3% reason code (20/24 labelled) on 33 hand-written varied cases. Safety: 0 unsafe auto-resolutions in both.
23. **Why not run Gemini evaluation?** No key or quota in the build sandbox. The scripts take `--provider gemini` unchanged, so it is the next step.
24. **How many tests?** 413 backend and 67 frontend tests passed on the latest code.

**Security and reliability**

25. **How is access controlled?** Reviewers use server-issued keys (reviewer name comes from the server, fails closed). Customers register or log in with scrypt-hashed passwords and signed session tokens; the portal only returns the customer's own transactions and complaints. It is prototype auth, not SSO or MFA.
26. **How do you protect PII?** Regex masking of emails, UPI IDs, card numbers (last 4 kept), mobiles, Aadhaar, PAN, CVV and OTP values before any model call, log or audit. It is best effort; names and addresses are not masked, and raw text is stored unmasked in the database.
27. **Can the same transaction be refunded twice?** The pipeline's route step takes a per-transaction lock and checks for other disputes on the same transaction; others force human review. Verified with threads on SQLite; the Postgres advisory-lock path is implemented but not exercised against a real Postgres.
28. **What about deadlines?** Computed in Python as of the dispute's received date, so re-runs are reproducible and customers aren't penalised for analyst delay. The model never computes dates.

**Business and next steps**

29. **How is this different from a chatbot?** A chatbot answers; DisputeIQ runs an audited investigation with deterministic policy and human approval, producing a reviewer-ready case.
30. **What would you build next?** SSO and MFA, live-model evaluation, network-specific rules and representment, real notification delivery, payment-system hooks with approvals, and a semantic embedder for similar-case retrieval.

## 4. Final cheat sheet

**One-liners**
- AI understands and drafts. Code decides. Human approves.
- The model can lower confidence, never change the action.
- No payment executed anywhere: it recommends.

**Numbers you can quote**
- 9 pipeline steps; 8 reason codes; 120-day window (ATM: 30 days).
- Auto-resolve: confidence >= 0.85, amount <= INR 10,000, duplicate or ATM only.
- 413 backend tests, 67 frontend tests.
- Offline evals: 99% (100 synthetic, circular); 83.3% reason code on 33 hand-written; 0 unsafe auto-resolutions in both.
- Rate limits: 10 login attempts per minute per email; 30 submits per minute per reviewer.

**Concepts**
- *LangGraph:* state graph; each node is a step and writes an audit event.
- *Structured output:* the model's reply is parsed against a Pydantic schema.
- *Placeholders:* letters use `{amount}`-style tokens filled from the database.
- *Friendly fraud:* "not me" claim on a strongly authenticated transaction.
- *Prompt injection:* complaint text trying to instruct the model. Mitigated structurally.
- *pgvector:* similar-case retrieval on Postgres only; default embedder is lexical hashing, not semantic.
- *Alembic:* schema migrations. *scrypt:* password hashing.

**Do not say**
- "99% accurate" without "offline, circular".
- "Gemini accuracy" of any kind.
- "Sends an email to the customer" (it's a portal draft).
- "Production-ready" or "SSO".
- Anything about the live site you haven't checked yourself.

**Be ready to admit**
- Free-tier cold start; Render database expires after 30 days.
- Regex PII masking and injection detection are heuristic.
- Last-4 digit filter typo in the frontend (server still validates).
